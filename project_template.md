# [Project Name]

*Last updated: [Date]*

> This file is context about a project you're working on. Fill in the core sections first — you can flesh out the optional ones over time.

---

## What Is This Project?
*One or two sentences at the elevator level. If someone stopped you in the hallway and asked "what are you working on?", this is what you'd say.*

## Why Does It Matter?
*What changes in the world — or in your organization — if this succeeds? Who benefits? What problem gets solved?*

## Current Status
*One line. Pick a state — on track, at risk, blocked, paused — and add a short phrase. Resist the urge to write a paragraph. This section gets overwritten as things change.*

## What Success Looks Like
*The realistic, achievable version. What does "we did it" actually mean? Be concrete enough that you'd know it when you see it.*

## What Wild Success Looks Like
*The stretch version. What does "this went better than we dared hope" look like? Don't be modest — articulating the upside is useful even if you don't expect to reach it.*

## Owner / Decision-Maker
*Who is actually accountable for this project, and who can approve, reshape, or kill it? This is often a different person from the one running the day-to-day work. Be specific: name the person who could shut this down if they wanted to.*

## Key Stakeholders and Where They Stand
*The short list of people who matter for this project. For each, describe their stance on this specific project — supportive, skeptical, undecided, actively blocking. This may differ from their general character (that's in their stakeholder file).*

## Key Risks or Blockers
*The one or two things most likely to derail this in the near term. Not theoretical risks — real ones you can name.*

## Expected Path
*Your current mental model of how this unfolds from here. Rough shape, not a detailed plan. Include any key dates or checkpoints you know, but don't sweat the middle steps. This is a snapshot of how you see it today — it will change.*

## What I Need From Each Stakeholder
*Short and specific. For each person in your key stakeholders list: what's the concrete thing you need from them, and by when?*

## Dependencies
*What has to be true, or what other teams, systems, or projects need to happen, for this to work? External things you don't control but rely on.*

## Next Critical Decisions
*Decisions coming up soon. For each: what's the decision, who makes it, and by when? This is distinct from assumptions — these are explicit choice points you're about to face.*

## How You'll Know It's Working
*How will you and your organization measure whether this is succeeding? Include baseline numbers if you have them. If the project doesn't have clean metrics, describe the signals you'll watch for instead.*

## Known Unknowns
*What are you still unsure about? Things you don't know that you wish you did. Naming these explicitly helps Claude reason with appropriate uncertainty rather than overconfidence.*

## Constraints and Non-Negotiables
*What you can't do, can't use, or can't change. Budget caps, timeline hard stops, vendors that are off-limits, approvals already locked in. These shape every recommendation and they're things nobody else would know.*

## Assumptions I'm Making
*What you're treating as given for this to work. "I'm assuming the CRM data will be clean enough." "I'm assuming Marcus's team will have capacity in May." Different from Known Unknowns — these are things you've quietly decided to rely on without proving.*

## What's Not In Scope
*The edges of the project. What are you deliberately not doing, even though it's tempting? Keeping this explicit helps you defend against scope creep.*

## What's Been Tried Before
*History that affects this project. Previous attempts, related initiatives that failed, lessons learned from similar work. This is the kind of context nobody can infer from the outside.*
